Free play time, subscriptions and the numbers to watch

BriteKind gives every family 30 minutes of total free play, then asks a parent to subscribe. It is a simple model with some technical details that decide whether it converts or annoys. Here is how it works and how we would measure it.

ShoutEx Team · Updated October 2026 · Page 5 of 5
BriteKind / Free play and subscriptions

How does the 30-minute free play model work?

The app counts active play time across sessions. Settings shows how many free minutes remain, with a Subscribe option behind the parent gate. When the 30 minutes are used, the camera stops, the dock disappears and a message asks a parent to subscribe to keep playing. Time is the meter because it maps directly to the value: more drawing sessions.

ModelHow it feels to a parentWatch out for
Time-limited free play (BriteKind)Fair: the child gets real play before any askCount only active play, not time with the app open in the background
Feature-limited free tierClear, but the free version can feel thinHard to choose which features to lock in a small app
Paid upfrontNo surprises, but no try-before-you-buyLower install volume from search
One-time unlock in-appSimple for a single-purpose toyNo recurring revenue to fund updates

What does the subscription need technically?

Use StoreKit with auto-renewable subscriptions and check entitlement on launch and when the app returns to the foreground. Store the free-minute counter in the keychain as well as user defaults, so a reinstall does not quietly reset the trial. The purchase button lives behind the parent gate, as the Kids Category requires.

If you add a free trial on top of the free minutes, Apple's guide to implementing introductory offers explains eligibility checks, so returning users do not see an offer they cannot claim.

Important

Show the price, the renewal period and how to cancel on the paywall itself. Unclear subscription terms are a common rejection reason and an even more common cause of refund requests from parents.

How should the free time end?

End it gently. A child mid-drawing should not see the screen go dark without warning. Show a short countdown in the last minute, let the current drawing finish and offer to save a screenshot if the parent has turned screenshots on. Then show the parent-facing message. The goal is that the child remembers the fun, not the interruption.

The American Academy of Pediatrics' statement on digital advertising to children is a useful reminder of where the line is: persuasive design aimed at young children draws criticism. Address the purchase message to the parent, not the child.

Founder rule

Never sell to the child.

The paywall talks to the parent, sits behind the gate and uses plain words. A kids app that pressures children to ask for a purchase will lose parents' trust faster than it gains revenue.

Which numbers show the model is working?

Track the funnel from install to paying family, and the shape of free play inside it. The most useful early signals are how many families use all 30 minutes, how many days that takes and how many parents who reach the end message go on to subscribe.

MetricWhat it tells you
Camera permission granted on first launchWhether onboarding explains the camera well enough
Families reaching 10, 20 and 30 free minutesWhether the core loop holds attention
Days to use the 30 minutesWhether play is a habit or a one-off novelty
End message to subscriptionWhether the paywall copy and price work
Month-two subscription retentionWhether families keep playing after the novelty

Our guide on paywalls for mobile apps covers paywall design, placement and testing in more detail. For honest numbers from other small-app founders on trial length and conversion, Indie Hackers is a useful place to compare notes, as long as you treat what you read as anecdote rather than benchmark.

Key point

If many families never reach 30 minutes, fix the play experience before touching the price. A paywall cannot convert families who stopped playing.
