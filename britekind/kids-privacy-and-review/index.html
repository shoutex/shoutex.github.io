Kids app privacy, permissions and App Review

A children's app that uses the camera starts with two strikes against it in review: a young audience and a sensitive permission. BriteKind's brief handles both with a parent gate, on-device processing and careful permission prompts. Here is the technical checklist.

BriteKind / Kids privacy and App Review

Which App Review rules apply to a kids app?

Apps for young children are held to stricter rules than general apps. Apple's App Review Guidelines require apps in the Kids Category to keep links out of the app, purchase opportunities and other distractions behind a parental gate, and they limit third-party analytics and advertising. Plan for this in the architecture, because removing an SDK late is harder than never adding it.

How strong should the parent gate be?

The brief specifies a math question with two random numbers from 1 to 5, one wrong answer allowed and a new question after two misses. That is a clear, testable spec. Our one change would be to make the question harder: many six-year-olds can add two numbers under five, so a multiplication question or numbers written as words keeps the gate closed for the whole target age.

The gate is only reachable by touch on the logo. If the flashlight lands on the logo, the app plays a click and shakes the icon but opens nothing. That small detail stops the light-driven interface from becoming a back door into settings.

How should camera and photo permissions be requested?

BriteKind needs the camera to work at all, and the Photos library only if a parent turns screenshots on. Ask for each at the moment it is needed, with a plain explanation in the Info.plist usage strings (NSCameraUsageDescription and NSPhotoLibraryAddUsageDescription). For screenshots, request add-only access, so the app can save images without ever reading the family's photo library. Apple's WWDC session on the limited Photos library explains the access levels introduced in iOS 14.

  • Check camera access on every launch. Parents can revoke it in Settings at any time, and the brief shows a clear "access to the camera is needed" screen until it is granted.
  • Grey out Screenshot when Photos access is missing. A disabled icon is better than a button that silently fails.
  • Never ask on first launch for something optional. A Photos prompt before the child has drawn anything is a likely "Don't Allow".

Our guide on permission prompts in mobile apps covers timing and wording in more depth, including pre-prompt screens that raise acceptance.

Does children's privacy law apply?

If an app is directed at children under 13 and collects personal information, the United States Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule applies. The FTC's six-step COPPA compliance plan is a practical starting point. BriteKind's design keeps the exposure small: no accounts, no names, no images leave the device, and feedback goes by email from the parent's own mail app.

Important

This is general product guidance, not legal advice. Children's privacy rules differ by country and change over time, so have a privacy lawyer review the app and its store listing before launch.

Founder rule

Design the data you never collect.

The easiest kids app to approve, and to defend, is the one that has nothing to leak. On-device processing and no accounts did more for BriteKind's privacy than any policy page could.
